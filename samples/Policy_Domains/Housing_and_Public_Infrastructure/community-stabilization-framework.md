# Community Stabilization — Neighborhood Conditions Framework

---

## Executive Summary

Safety is a function of environment, not only enforcement. Decades of criminological research establish that the physical and social conditions of a neighborhood are among the most powerful predictors of whether violence occurs and whether communities can recover from it. Lighting, maintained public space, active ground-floor uses, staffed third spaces, and visible investment change daily behavior. Their absence — vacancy, deterioration, darkness, and systematic disinvestment — signals that no one is watching, that the community does not matter, and that the informal social accountability that holds most neighborhoods together has been withdrawn.

This framework establishes federal support for targeted neighborhood condition improvements in communities that have been systematically underfunded, over-policed, and asked to wait for the long-term systems reforms that will eventually address the structural roots of crime. It does not replace those long-term systems. It changes what those communities experience right now, while the larger transformation is underway.

The intervention model is environmental and social, not punitive. It repairs places, activates spaces, and creates the physical and social conditions for collective efficacy to take hold. It is explicitly not a broken-windows enforcement program. The lesson of broken-windows research is that deteriorated environments suppress social accountability. The appropriate response is to repair the environment — not to arrest the people living in it.

---

## Why Environment Matters

The criminological case for environmental investment rests on three bodies of research that have accumulated over decades and across countries.

**Collective efficacy** — the willingness of neighbors to intervene on behalf of the common good, and the trust that makes such intervention feel worthwhile — is among the strongest predictors of neighborhood safety (Sampson et al., 1997). Collective efficacy is not a cultural trait that some communities have and others lack. It is a structural outcome. It develops where people know each other, where public space is worth spending time in, where the expectation of social order is credible, and where intervention feels like it will be supported rather than punished. Environmental investment builds the physical preconditions for collective efficacy to develop. Disinvestment destroys them. *Note: the causal chain from environmental improvement to collective efficacy to crime reduction is supported in the broader criminological literature (Sampson; Wilson and Kelling; Kondo et al.) but is not what the 17A report itself demonstrates. The 17A evidence supports the near-term crime-reduction effects of specific environmental interventions. The collective efficacy mechanism is a theoretical bridge from that evidence to longer-term community formation outcomes, drawn from a separate body of research.*

**Environmental criminology** — particularly the work on Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design — demonstrates that lighting, sight lines, activation of public space, and removal of vacancy consistently reduce crime and fear of crime. These effects are not marginal. Improved street lighting has been associated in a rigorous natural experiment with a **39% reduction in outdoor nighttime crime** in NYC public housing.[^9] Greening vacant lots — cleanup, grass, basic landscaping — reduced nearby gun violence by **29%** in a Philadelphia randomized controlled trial.[^10] The mechanism is straightforward: visibility raises the perceived risk of criminal behavior and enables the informal social monitoring that resident presence provides. A well-lit, active block is not just more pleasant. It is structurally safer.

**Third spaces and youth environments** — community centers, recreation facilities, staffed drop-in programs, neighborhood commercial anchors — reduce crime by providing alternatives to idleness and isolation, particularly for young people during high-risk hours. Their absence is not a neutral condition. It is a structural feature of underinvested neighborhoods that increases exposure to risk by eliminating the legitimate alternatives. *Note: the evidence base for staffed third spaces as a crime-reduction mechanism is more heterogeneous than the evidence for physical environmental interventions such as lighting and vacant lot remediation. Effect sizes vary across program types and contexts. The claim here is that third spaces are a necessary complement to environmental investment — not that their effect sizes are equivalent or as consistently demonstrated.*

The evidence base is sufficient to justify investment at scale. What it does not justify is punitive enforcement or surveillance dressed up as environmental intervention. The goal is to change the conditions. The conditions are what change behavior.

## Core Design Principles

**Fix environments, not people.** The intervention target is the physical and social conditions of the neighborhood. Criminalization of the symptoms of environmental neglect — loitering, public presence, informal commerce — reproduces the harm it claims to address. Environmental investment addresses the actual problem.

**Visible investment communicates something enforcement cannot.** In communities where the primary experience of the state is enforcement, visible infrastructure investment — repaired lighting, maintained public space, a staffed community center that is actually open — communicates that this community is valued, that the investment is sustained, and that residents deserve the same public environment as wealthier neighborhoods. This is not symbolic. It is how trust in public institutions is built or rebuilt.

**Activate, don't surveil.** The mechanism through which environmental investment improves safety is social presence and natural accountability — neighbors in public space, eyes on the street, community members who know each other. This is categorically different from cameras, police visibility, or formal monitoring. These mechanisms should not be conflated, and surveillance infrastructure should not be introduced under the banner of community investment.

**Connect every intervention to the broader stabilization system.** A repaired corridor that connects to nothing is less valuable than one that connects to a crisis response team, a workforce stabilization pathway, a healthcare-enabled housing pilot. Every investment in this framework is mapped to downstream system connections so that individuals who engage with improved neighborhood environments are routed toward durable stability, not just temporarily better surroundings.

## System Components

### 1. Lighting and Physical Corridor Repair

The most immediately visible form of neighborhood disinvestment is physical: broken lighting, unmaintained sidewalks, deteriorated corridors, accumulated blight that signals abandonment. These conditions have direct documented effects on perceived safety, pedestrian behavior, and crime rates. They are also the fastest to address.

Targeted lighting upgrades — prioritizing high-density pedestrian corridors, transit connections, and commercial strips — represent among the highest-return per-dollar investments available in community safety. Federal support funds rapid-deployment lighting assessments and upgrades in identified high-risk corridors, with maintenance funding attached so improvements do not decay within two years. Assessment and prioritization use DoDA neighborhood-condition dashboards, which track environmental indicators alongside crisis response density and other stabilization inputs, so investment is targeted where the gap between conditions and need is greatest.

**Targeting is adaptive, not static.** Research on geographic crime concentration finds that in a typical city, 28–49% of the highest-concentration micro-areas are different from one year to the next — concentration churns significantly.[^11] Priority location lists are recalculated weekly against updated DoDA data. Sites remain on the active list until environmental conditions have stabilized across multiple retargeting cycles, not until the first intervention is complete. The targeting logic prioritizes **emerging-deterioration areas** (places trending in the wrong direction, typically just 1–3% of city geography) over chronic high-crime areas where conditions are severe but stable — because marginal intervention has the greatest impact where trajectories are still changeable.

Lighting and corridor repair is coordinated with STZ corridor planning where applicable so near-term physical improvements align with long-term public realm investment. They function as the leading edge of a sustained buildout rather than isolated repairs that become stranded when the corridor redevelops around them.

The visible repair of corridors is designed to be completed within 90 days of program activation in participating neighborhoods. The speed of visible improvement matters. Communities that have been told investment is coming and watched it not arrive have earned their skepticism. The first deliverable is something you can see from the street within three months.

### 2. Third Spaces and Youth-Accessible Environments

A third space is any place that is not home and not work — a library reading room, a community center gym, a staffed youth program, a neighborhood café with evening hours. Third spaces are where social life happens, where trust between neighbors develops, and where young people have a real alternative to the street during high-risk hours. Their presence in a neighborhood is not a luxury. Their absence is a structural condition that increases risk.

Federal support funds three things here. First, **staffed third spaces in high-need corridors**: priority investment in community centers, recreation facilities, and multi-use spaces that are open during evening and weekend hours — the hours when the absence of alternatives is most consequential — staffed by trained personnel and accessible without cost or membership barriers. Existing facilities are activated and extended first; new construction is funded where gaps cannot be addressed through existing infrastructure.

Second, **youth programming during high-risk hours**: structured, paid engagement for young people during the windows — after school, evenings, weekends — when unsupervised time and exposure to risk are highest. These programs are not punitive or surveillance-oriented. They are genuine alternatives: workforce skill-building, arts, athletics, mentorship, and paid service opportunities that connect to the broader workforce stabilization and violence interruption system.

Third, **alignment with the Neighborhood Civic Overlay framework**: the NCO creates the regulatory pathway for by-right small-scale community services in residential neighborhoods. This framework provides the startup capital and operating support for the first deployment cycle in high-need corridors, so the regulatory permission and the funding arrive together rather than one without the other.

### 3. Activation of Vacant and Unsafe Areas

Vacant lots and abandoned buildings are not neutral. They are environments of elevated risk — sites of illegal dumping, drug activity, and violence — that communicate disinvestment to everyone who passes by them. Research on vacant lot remediation, including rigorous studies of Philadelphia's greening program, demonstrates consistent reductions in gun violence, stress, and fear of crime in surrounding areas when lots are cleaned, greened, and maintained. The effect size is meaningful. This is not a marginal intervention.

Federal support funds rapid remediation of vacant lots in identified high-risk corridors: cleanup, greening, and basic activation as usable public space. The model is maintenance-sustained: sites are funded through ongoing maintenance commitments, tracked through DoDa, and evaluated against crime and well-being outcomes over a multi-year horizon.

Vacant commercial space receives a parallel intervention: temporary activation through pop-up programming, community markets, and transitional uses that create foot traffic and social presence in corridors where vacancy has produced dead zones. Temporary activation is coordinated with long-term housing and commercial development pipelines so it functions as a bridge to permanent use rather than a substitute for it.

### 4. Local Commercial and Community Presence

Neighborhood commercial anchors — grocery stores, hardware stores, pharmacies, barbershops, restaurants — do something that public programs cannot. They create the daily economic and social life that makes a neighborhood feel inhabited and worth investing in. Their absence, which characterizes many disinvested urban neighborhoods, is not a market failure that will self-correct. It is the result of decades of disinvestment, redlining, and the removal of the purchasing power that would have made those businesses viable.

This framework supports targeted commercial corridor investment in neighborhoods where the community stabilization assessment identifies commercial vacancy as a primary condition driver. Support includes small business technical assistance, gap financing through PCA consortium instruments where applicable, and coordination with the workforce stabilization system so that new neighborhood businesses connect to local hiring pipelines rather than importing labor from outside the community.

The goal is not to manufacture commercial districts where none is viable. It is to close the gap between the demand that exists in underinvested neighborhoods and the supply that has been systematically withheld from them.

## System Integration

The community stabilization framework does not stand alone. It functions as the environmental layer of the broader stabilization system, and its connections to other components are explicit rather than aspirational.

*Evidence note: the existing research base (including 17A's Dallas case study) describes city-government implementation models operating under mayoral leadership with locally-embedded analytical partners. Whether federal coordination improves, complicates, or is neutral in its effect on this type of locally-dependent coordination is not addressed in the current literature. The assumption that federal involvement via DoDA and the Execution Corps enhances rather than displaces local ownership is a design hypothesis, not a demonstrated finding, and requires separate sourcing in a future Phase 3 research pass.*

Crisis response infrastructure and community stabilization are designed together. DoDa tracks crisis response density by neighborhood, and that data drives community stabilization targeting — the neighborhoods with the highest crisis contact rates receive priority for environmental investment, on the logic that crisis response and environmental improvement are addressing the same underlying conditions from different angles. A well-lit corridor with an active community center and a responsive 988 dispatch system is a qualitatively different environment than any one of those interventions alone.

The violence interruption and youth off-ramps framework depends on community stabilization having created the physical and social infrastructure within which interruption workers can operate. Credible messengers working in deteriorated, socially atomized environments face structural obstacles that environmental investment removes. The sequence matters: environment first, then targeted intervention, because targeted intervention is more effective in an environment that supports it.

Long-term housing and transit investment through the STZ framework is the destination this layer is pointing toward. Community stabilization investments are sited and designed in anticipation of the long-term corridor buildout so they function as the leading edge of sustained investment rather than isolated improvements that become incongruous when the neighborhood redevelops around them.

## Performance Metrics

**Safety:** Crime rate trends in targeted corridors; fear of crime indicators from resident surveys; crisis response call density over time in treated areas.

**Environment:** Lighting coverage and maintenance status; vacant lot and commercial vacancy rates; third space utilization and hours of operation across the week.

**Equity:** Investment distribution relative to need indicators; time from program activation to visible corridor improvement; resident satisfaction with public environment.

**System integration:** Warm handoff rate from third space staff and programming to crisis response, workforce, and housing pathways; downstream stabilization outcomes for individuals who engaged with community programming.

## Failure Modes

**Investments without maintenance** are the most common failure mode in community investment programs. A repaired corridor that degrades within three years because maintenance funding was not attached communicates something worse than the original condition: that improvement was temporary and the community was not worth sustaining. Maintenance funding is a design requirement, not an afterthought.

**Activation without connection** produces temporary improvements that do not change underlying trajectories. A community center that is not connected to workforce pathways, healthcare access, and housing stabilization is a room people visit rather than a front door to a system that can actually change their circumstances. Every programmatic element in this framework carries explicit downstream connection requirements.

**Investment without community ownership** produces environments that feel imposed rather than claimed. Physical improvements that do not engage residents in their design and ongoing management do not generate the collective efficacy they are intended to support. Community input and resident employment in the maintenance and operation of improved spaces are operational requirements, not optional features.

**Surveillance creep** — the gradual introduction of monitoring infrastructure under the banner of safety investment — is the failure mode that most directly damages the trust this framework is designed to rebuild. Camera networks, predictive policing systems, and data-sharing arrangements with law enforcement are explicitly excluded from this framework and cannot be introduced as conditions of funding.

## Execution Corps Deployment — Crime Reduction Council

The environmental coordination model described in this framework requires an institutional vehicle capable of holding weekly cross-agency accountability without becoming a new bureaucracy. Research from 17A's Dallas implementation (2024–2025) demonstrates that the central bottleneck is not resources or legal authority — cities already have both — but a standing coordination mechanism that identifies where to act, tracks follow-through, and adapts weekly as conditions shift.[^12] The coordination model works. The constraint is having a body that maintains the rhythm.

The policy platform operationalizes this through an **Execution Corps Crime Reduction Council (CRC)** deployment: a time-bounded federal coordination unit that establishes the weekly cross-agency rhythm in participating cities, transfers the model to local institutions, and exits once the coordination function is self-sustaining.

### CRC Structure

The CRC is not a new agency. It is a coordination layer deployed over existing city departments — code enforcement, sanitation, public works, parks, and transportation — with a defined scope (crime reduction in DoDA-identified priority corridors), a defined sunset trigger (see below), and a bounded budget that funds the coordination function, not the underlying agency operations.

The personnel requirements below mirror 17A's "What you need" framework directly, with the specific Dallas configuration as the reference model.[^12]

**1. Mayoral or city-manager commitment.** The coordination model only moves if city leadership signals — directly to department heads — that they will be expected to prioritize specific high-violence locations and report on progress. Without that signal, coordination meetings become optional and follow-through fades.[^12] This is a prerequisite for CRC deployment, not an outcome of it.

**2. CRC Coordinator** (one person; existing city staff or EC deployment; *not* a new hire): owns the weekly rhythm — scheduling meetings, tracking assignments, flagging stalled cases, and connecting DoDA analytical targeting with operational execution. In Dallas, this role sat in the Emergency Management and Crisis Response office; viable alternatives include the mayor's office, the city manager's office, or a public safety coordinator role. The title is not the constraint. What matters is that one person wakes up every Monday thinking about which sites need attention that week.[^12] In the CRC model, this is the EC deployment role that transitions to city staff at sunset.

**3. Two to three invested agency leads** (existing department staff; *not* new hires): the minimum table to start is **code enforcement plus one or two others** — sanitation, public works, or transportation — with leads who are willing to show up weekly and do the work. The Dallas model expanded over time: code enforcement brought representatives from multiple divisions (field inspections, nuisance abatement, multi-tenant housing) because the work touched each; parks and additional public works divisions joined as the rhythm proved its value. Start with a small, committed group. The table grows as results accumulate.[^12]

**4. DoDA Liaison** (analytical function; may be remote or embedded): provides the data targeting layer — neighborhood-condition dashboard access, weekly site-list generation, and metric reporting. In Dallas, CPAL served this role: "CPAL's program personnel facilitated the coordination meetings and synthesized field intelligence to update the targeting each week."[^12] In the platform model, this function is the DoDA pilot team, which provides the same analytical infrastructure without requiring cities to build or procure a CPAL-equivalent independently.

**5. Homelessness EC Connector** (shared position, activated only on pattern-detected combined-signal sites — see Combined-Signal Sites below): this role is *not* at the table by default and is *not* added on first flag. The connector is activated by the LLM pattern-detection layer after a recurring co-occurrence has been confirmed across multiple meeting cycles.

### Weekly Operational Cadence

The weekly cadence is the core mechanism of this model — not the technology, not the funding, not the legal authority. 17A's Dallas case analysis found that allowing the cadence to slip from weekly to bi-weekly or monthly is the primary failure mode across comparable implementations.[^12] The specific rhythm is:

**Monday — Targeting and Resource Allocation**

- CRC Coordinator pulls updated DoDA neighborhood-condition dashboard
- Priority location list recalculated against current crime incident data, code violations, 311 complaint volume, and environmental condition indicators
- Sites ranked by: (a) emerging-deterioration score (areas trending in the wrong direction, as distinct from chronic stable hotspots) and (b) environmental intervention feasibility (whether city departments can act on identified conditions this week)
- Resource allocation confirmed with agency leads; blockers surfaced to the RMC standing channel
- Combined-signal flags reviewed for new or escalating sites where DoDA HSEWI indicators co-locate with crime concentration

**Tuesday — Department Follow-Up and Case Tracking**

- Facilitated meeting with agency leads: status of each active site; completed interventions confirmed; stalled cases identified; assignments for the coming week set
- Stalled cases (3+ consecutive cycles without intervention) elevated to RMC for regulatory or resource resolution
- Meeting auto-transcribed; transcript processed by LLM integration to extract action items, assignments, and site status updates (see Technology Stack below)
- Weekly progress report auto-generated against DoDA performance benchmarks; distributed to agency leads and posted to DoDA dashboard

**Wednesday–Friday — Field Deployment**

- Agency departments execute assigned interventions: lighting repairs, code enforcement actions, lot remediation, sanitation response, public realm activation
- CRC Coordinator conducts at least one joint field visit per week to priority sites
- Field observations logged to shared site-tracking record in DoDA

### Technology Stack

The CRC's administrative infrastructure is designed to minimize the coordination overhead that causes similar programs to atrophy. The three components:

**1. Meeting platform with auto-transcription.** Any enterprise meeting platform with automatic transcription capability is sufficient (Teams, Zoom, Google Meet, or equivalent). Requirements: auto-transcription of every meeting; transcripts stored in a shared record accessible to the CRC Coordinator and all agency leads; calendar integration so meeting invites auto-generate the shared agenda template with the standard site-review format.

**2. LLM integration for action-item extraction, tracking, and pattern detection.** Meeting transcripts are processed by an LLM integration that extracts: action items with assigned owner and target date; site status updates (new, active, stalled, resolved); blocker flags for RMC escalation; cadence deviations (missed meetings, absent agency leads). Output is auto-populated to a shared case management record in DoDA and contributes to the weekly progress report. This eliminates manual agenda-preparation and follow-up tracking — the two administrative burdens most likely to cause coordination programs to degrade over time.

The LLM layer also runs a **pattern-detection pass** across the rolling transcript archive. When a site appears in consecutive meeting records as unresolved and the transcript language co-occurs with indicators of housing instability (encampment references, 311 activity, acute shelter need), the pattern-detection layer flags the site as a candidate combined-signal site and — after the pattern is confirmed across multiple meeting cycles — auto-generates a calendar invite for the Homelessness EC Connector for the next Tuesday meeting. This is a pull mechanism, not a push: the connector enters the meeting only when a recurring, confirmed pattern warrants it. (See Combined-Signal Sites below for full protocol.)

**3. DoDA data backend.** DoDA's neighborhood-condition dashboard provides the analytical targeting layer. The Monday retargeting pull is a standardized query against updated DoDA indicators; output is a ranked site list with intervention-type recommendations. In pilot cities with an active 17A analytical relationship, the 17A analytical methodology is used for initial site-selection validation — specifically, CPAL's multi-indicator composite with a stability-analysis filter that distinguishes genuine deterioration from small-number statistical noise using Poisson-based methods.

*On predictive targeting as a future upgrade path:* 17A explicitly does not use a predictive model and recommends against treating one as a prerequisite: "You don't need a predictive model, a risk-terrain analysis, or a proprietary platform to get started."[^12] The base CRC implementation uses the same data-driven ranking and stability-analysis approach validated in Dallas — accessible to any city with geocoded crime data, and manageable for practitioners trained in the 17A/CPAL methodology.

The DoDA architecture is designed to accommodate a predictive layer as an optional plug-in upgrade: as DoDA accumulates multi-city longitudinal data across pilot deployments, the tipping-point identification task (is this half-mile area deteriorating or stable?) is a binary classification problem on structured tabular data — crime incident rates, code violation density, 311 complaint volume, environmental condition indicators, and trend history. The appropriate model type will be determined by the DoDA data structure as it matures across cities; gradient boosting methods tend to perform well on tabular classification problems of this kind, though the architecture is designed to accommodate whichever classification approach best fits the accumulated data. A binary classifier of this type is preferable to a large generative model — lower inference cost, lower distribution drift risk, more auditable decision logic, and interpretable feature importance that 17A-style analysts can act on directly. The classifier slots into the existing Monday retargeting pull without requiring any change to the coordination model itself. Analytical sophistication grows after the coordination rhythm is working — never before, and never as a prerequisite for a city to begin.

### Sunset Conditions

A CRC unit exits a city when three conditions are met simultaneously:

1. The weekly coordination cadence has been maintained for at least 18 consecutive months, with city staff running the final 6 months without EC facilitation
2. DoDA priority site count for the city falls below the activation threshold on 4 of the prior 6 monthly assessments
3. The city has formally committed to sustaining the CRC Coordinator position and DoDA data infrastructure after EC departure

At sunset, the CRC Coordinator role is expected to convert to a city-funded position. The technology stack (meeting platform, LLM integration, DoDA access) transitions to a city maintenance contract.

## Pilot Design — 5-City Launch

The CRC operational model is pilotable immediately using DoDA's pilot team and, in eligible cities, 17A's existing analytical infrastructure. The pilot tests three things: (a) whether the coordination model can be stood up in 90 days using existing city staff; (b) whether visible corridor improvement is achievable within 180 days; and (c) whether the meeting-software/LLM integration sustains the weekly cadence without requiring a dedicated CPAL-scale analytical organization.

### City Selection Criteria

Pilot cities are selected against four criteria:

1. **Scale.** ≥ 500 annual violent crimes identifiable in 10–30 half-mile concentration areas — sufficient for the coordination model to be meaningful and outcomes to be measurable
2. **Analytical capacity.** Existing crime data infrastructure at the police department level; willingness to share data with the DoDA pilot team OR a prior relationship with CPAL or an equivalent analytical partner
3. **Institutional commitment.** Written mayoral or city-manager commitment to the weekly coordination cadence; at least one confirmed agency lead from code enforcement, sanitation, public works, parks, or transportation
4. **Complementarity.** At least one pilot city should have an active 17A advisory relationship, so that 17A's analytical models and analysts can be borrowed for the initial site-selection pass and operational matching, at no additional cost to the pilot budget

Pilot cities are expected to represent geographic diversity and include at least two cities below 500,000 population, to evaluate whether the model scales across city typologies.

### Pilot Timeline

- **Months 1–3:** CRC Coordinator embedded; site list established; Monday/Tuesday cadence initiated; technology stack configured; first combined-signal site flags reviewed
- **Months 3–6:** First measurable outcomes tracked against DoDA benchmarks; homelessness handoff protocol in active use where applicable
- **Months 6–18:** EC progressively reduces facilitation role; city staff take increasing ownership; CRC Coordinator begins transition to city-funded status
- **Month 18 evaluation:** Sunset assessment; cities meeting conditions transition to self-sustaining model; cities not yet meeting conditions receive an additional 12-month support cycle with revised transition plan

### Evidence Collected

- Site-level crime rate trends in targeted corridors (DoDA weekly tracking, pre/post comparison)
- Time from program activation to first visible corridor improvement (target: 90 days)
- Weekly cadence maintenance rate (number of weeks the Monday/Tuesday rhythm held)
- Administrative burden reduction (coordinator time on agenda-prep and tracking, pre/post LLM integration)
- Homelessness handoff protocol: number of combined-signal sites identified; time to dual-deployment activation; housing placement rate for individuals at combined-signal sites

## Combined-Signal Sites: Violence and Housing Instability Handoff Protocol

When DoDA's neighborhood-condition dashboard identifies a CRC priority site that also carries active housing instability indicators — persistent homeless encampment presence, elevated HSEWI score, or field-confirmed co-occurrence of violence and acute shelter need — the site is classified as a **combined-signal site** and triggers a dual-deployment protocol.

This protocol addresses a gap identified in the Dallas case study: a single absentee-landlord property functioning as a homeless encampment required months of multi-agency environmental intervention under the crime reduction coordination model — coordination that would have been substantially reduced had the housing instability at that site been addressed through the homelessness prevention automatic stabilizer system.[^12] The platform design intention is that these systems interoperate. The protocol below specifies how.

### Detection

DoDA automatically flags combined-signal sites during the Monday targeting pull. Flag criteria: site appears in the top-50 crime concentration locations for the city AND carries either (a) an HSEWI score in the highest quartile for the city OR (b) active 311 encampment reports in the prior 30 days. A first-cycle flag is informational only — it is logged to the site record and appears in the Monday briefing, but does not automatically trigger the homelessness integration.

### Activation

The Homelessness EC Connector is activated by **LLM pattern detection**, not by a first-cycle flag or a standing calendar assignment. The LLM integration processing the Tuesday meeting transcripts runs a rolling pattern-detection pass: when a site appears in the transcript record as unresolved across multiple consecutive meeting cycles, and the meeting language co-occurs with housing instability indicators (encampment references, 311 encampment activity, HSEWI flag), the LLM flags the site as a confirmed combined-signal candidate and auto-generates a calendar invite for the Homelessness EC Connector for the following Tuesday meeting.

The threshold for activation is a confirmed recurring pattern — typically 2–3 consecutive meeting cycles where the site is discussed as unresolved with housing instability language present — not a single DoDA flag. This design keeps the Homelessness EC Connector out of meetings where it isn't warranted and avoids the coordination overhead of adding stakeholders on first contact with a problem that may resolve through environmental intervention alone.

### Dual Deployment

**CRC** retains ownership of the site for environmental intervention: lighting, code enforcement, lot remediation, infrastructure repair, and all physical corridor conditions.

**Homelessness EC** takes ownership of the housing instability dimension: outreach, intake, discharge planning, ERA routing, and healthcare-enabled housing connections for individuals at the site.

In pilot cities with an active 17A analytical relationship, 17A analysts participate in the initial combined-signal site assessment to provide operational matching guidance — distinguishing individuals at the site who are candidates for rapid housing placement from those requiring more intensive behavioral health engagement before housing can be stabilized.

**Shared site record.** Both EC units log to the same DoDA site-tracking record. Crime reduction metrics are the CRC's accountability. Housing placement metrics are the Homelessness EC's accountability. The DoDA dashboard surfaces both simultaneously.

### Resolution

A combined-signal site exits the dual-deployment protocol when both of the following conditions are met:

- The homelessness condition is resolved: no active encampment, HSEWI below threshold for 60 consecutive days
- The crime concentration condition is resolved: site no longer appears in the CRC priority list on 4 of 6 consecutive retargeting cycles

Either condition alone is insufficient. Resolving the environmental conditions without addressing housing instability produces a temporary improvement that is likely to recur at or near the same site. Resolving housing instability without addressing the physical environment leaves the crime concentration mechanism in place through a different pathway.

---

## Bottom Line

The communities this framework is designed to serve have not lacked attention from the criminal justice system. They have lacked investment in the conditions that make safety possible. The physical and social environment of a neighborhood is not background to the question of public safety. It is one of its primary determinants.

This framework repairs that environment — quickly, visibly, and with explicit connections to the broader stabilization system — so that communities experience real investment alongside the long-term systems reforms that will eventually address the structural roots of what they have been living with.

The first deliverable is something you can see from the street. The second is something you can feel in the neighborhood. The third — measured over years — is a community that is genuinely safer, not because enforcement increased, but because the conditions that produce violence were systematically addressed.
