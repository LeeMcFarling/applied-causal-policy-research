# Zoning for Function
## The Housing, Mobility & Work Ladder

## Governing Statement

Zoning exists to serve the people who live under it. It does not exist to preserve an abstract land-use taxonomy, to protect property values through exclusion, or to enforce a single acceptable image of what a neighborhood should look like.

This framework reforms zoning around **function**, meaning what a household actually needs to do in the course of an ordinary week: live somewhere stable, get to work, buy food, see a doctor, get their kids to school and childcare, see other people, and do all of this without every single trip requiring a car and a parking spot at both ends.

Postwar American zoning was not built around this question, nor was it built around any single household's daily life at all. It was built around separation (residential apart from commercial apart from industrial apart from civic) enforced at a scale and a rigidity that made daily function dependent on constant driving, made basic services illegal to build near housing, and made the missing-middle housing stock that used to exist between "single-family home" and "high-rise apartment" functionally illegal to build again. The instruments in this domain (the Neighborhood Civic Overlay, Special Transit Zones, Node Typologies, the Infrastructure Spine) exist because that separation broke the built environment's ability to serve the people living inside it.

This document states the zoning reform explicitly, as a graduated ladder, so it can be read, argued with, and tested against evidence on its own terms, separate from the individual instruments that implement each rung.

---

## The Core Principle: Function Before Form

A functional neighborhood lets a person meet most of their daily needs (housing, food, care, work, gathering) within a short walk, a short bike ride, a short transit trip, or a short drive, at their choice. It does this without requiring density everywhere, without banning single-family homes, and without mandating any particular architectural style.

The reform is not a mandate to "build density" everywhere, nor is it a bet that any single housing type solves affordability on its own. It is this: **make it legal again for a place to function**, and let the market, existing neighborhoods, and local jurisdictions decide where and how fast that happens through opt-in adoption, by-right compliance checklists, and the fiscal solvency tests already established elsewhere in this domain.

Zoning reform under this framework is judged by one question at every rung: **does this let people do more of what they need to do, closer to where they already live, without taking away what they have?**

## The Ladder

The ladder describes a sequence of land-use conditions, not a mandate that every neighborhood climb it. Adoption of the ladder is opt-in. Rung 0 is the minimum baseline once adopted; higher rungs activate only where the jurisdiction separately elects or qualifies to use them, each governed by its own instrument already defined elsewhere in this domain, and a neighborhood may sit permanently at Rung 0 if its residents choose to. Projects meeting the requirements of an activated rung proceed by right.

### Rung 0: The Single-Family Core, Seam-Compatible

**Default condition:** Nothing about the neighborhood's interior character or the housing itself changes. Lot sizes, setbacks, home types, and use restrictions on single-family parcels remain exactly as they are. What changes is the periphery: Rung 0 opens the seam, the edge of the subdivision, to walkable and bikeable daily-needs services and to Accessory Dwelling Units (ADUs), so residents gain access to those functions without any alteration inside the neighborhood itself.

Like every instrument in this domain, this activation is piloted first, evaluated against pre-registered thresholds, and scaled only once proven. A jurisdiction that opts in adopts the full Rung 0 baseline, not a menu it can partially select from, and reports its configuration publicly so residents and other jurisdictions can see exactly what was adopted and what it produced.

**What activates at this rung:**
- **ADUs** as the connective tissue between Rung 0 and Rung 1: a form of gentle, invisible-from-the-street density that lets a household add a rental unit, house an aging parent, or house a young adult child without materially changing the neighborhood's street-facing character or requiring a parcel-level rezoning. ADUs are legalized by right, subject to the same Local Infrastructure Funding Test (LIFT) and pattern-book form standards used elsewhere in this domain, wherever a jurisdiction has not opted into a higher rung.
- **Neighborhood Civic Overlay (NCO)** eligibility at the seam and edge of the subdivision: small-scale, by-right civic and commercial uses (cafés, childcare, clinics, small markets) sited at the boundary rather than inside the interior of the residential fabric, gated by the LIFT solvency test.

**Function served:** family stability, quiet enjoyment, intergenerational housing flexibility, and walkable access to a small number of daily-needs uses at the edge, without changing what the neighborhood is.

### Rung 1: Missing Middle Infill

**What activates:** duplexes, triplexes, fourplexes, courtyard and bungalow courts, and small townhome clusters, permitted by right within NCO-designated corridors and seam districts adjacent to Rung 0 neighborhoods. Structures remain within missing-middle scale (walkable, human-scaled, compatible in height and setback with the surrounding block) and must comply with the applicable regional architectural pattern book.

**Why this rung matters specifically:** many of America's most valued historic neighborhoods rely heavily on housing forms at this rung (Charleston single-houses, Chicago two-flats, California courtyard bungalows, Boston triple-deckers among them). It was zoned out of legality in most American jurisdictions over the last eight decades, not because it failed, but because a specific set of zoning instruments made it illegal to build again. Restoring it is a restoration of what already worked, not an import of something new.

**Function served:** starter homeownership, aging-in-place downsizing without leaving the neighborhood, workforce housing proximity to existing jobs, and a smoother price gradient between renting and owning than the current binary of "single-family house" or "large apartment complex."

**Fiscal gate:** LIFT applies. A project proceeds only where LIFT finds that existing capacity is sufficient, or that projected incremental public revenue and secured contributions cover its incremental infrastructure burden. Reused, already-sunk capacity counts toward sufficiency; the test measures marginal fiscal impact, not the project's raw assessed value.

---

### Rung 2: Corridor & Node Mid-Rise

**What activates:** the mid-rise density envelope already defined under Node Typologies and Special Transit Zones: five- to eight-story mixed-use blocks at designated nodes (transit stations, underutilized big-box and strip-commercial parcels, stadium and event parking fields), delivered through the Tenure-Convertible Mid-Rise Typology and financed through PCA-enabled consortium capital where private capital underperforms relative to need.

**Function served:** transit-supported household formation at scale, ground-floor retail and civic space serving as third places (see the theoretical grounding in the Built Environment & Community Pillars framework), and workforce housing directly connected to regional job centers.

This rung is where the existing instruments in this domain (STZ, Node Typologies, PCA financing, the Infrastructure Spine, Regional Pattern Books) already do the governing work. This ladder document does not restate their mechanics; it places them at the correct rung in the sequence.

---

### Rung 3: Transit-Anchored Mixed-Use, With Active Ground-Floor Function

**What this rung makes explicit that Rung 2 does not fully specify:** ground-floor function is mandatory at primary transit nodes and strongly weighted in the compliance checklist at secondary nodes, subject to documented market-feasibility and vacancy safeguards so the standard does not produce empty retail shells in markets that cannot yet sustain continuous commercial frontage. Ground-floor function is not limited to retail: childcare, clinics, community rooms, maker space, live-work units, and residential stoops all count as occupied, active frontage. A mid-rise residential block with a blank, inactive ground floor does not meet this rung's standard even if it meets the density envelope. The colonnade and public-realm standards already defined for the Infrastructure Spine (daylight, active edges, walkable frontage) apply to the ground floor of every building at this rung, not only to spine-adjacent structures.

**The car-inclusive design standard, stated explicitly:** parking minimums are reduced or eliminated at this rung, not because cars are unwelcome, but because the objective is redundant mobility. A resident should be able to live, work, and meet daily needs at this rung entirely on foot, by bike, or by transit, **or** by car, at their own choice. Surface parking is consolidated into structured parking rather than eliminated outright, consistent with the parking-consolidation approach already used in the Node Typologies and Colorado Pilot frameworks. This is not a policy against driving, and it is not indifferent to the households who depend on a car today. It is a policy against **requiring** driving as the only way to accomplish an ordinary day. A household that wants to keep two cars and drive everywhere retains that option at this rung; a household that does not want to depend on a car is, for the first time in most American zoning codes, permitted to build a life without one.

**Function served:** the full daily-needs radius – housing, work, food, care, retail, and civic life – reachable without mandatory car dependency, while preserving car access and parking capacity for those who choose it.

---

### Rung 4: Industrial & Work-Integration Zoning

**What activates:** zoning that reconnects housing to work, rather than assuming the two are always separated by a commute. This rung draws directly on the Industrial Transition Zone typology already identified in Node Typologies and on the coordination logic in the Urban Industrial Integration framework, and makes the residential-adjacent zoning question explicit:

- **Light-industrial and flex transition zones** adjacent to residential and mixed-use fabric, with buffering standards (noise, emissions, traffic separation) sufficient that proximity to work does not mean exposure to harm.
- **Live-work and workforce-adjacent housing** near industrial and manufacturing corridors, sequenced with the workforce coordination instruments (WCA, ISA) described in the labor and industrial domains, so that housing capacity is planned concurrently with industrial corridor designation rather than reactively after workers arrive. Residential adjacency may not create a pathway for later nuisance claims, rezoning pressure, or land-price escalation that displaces compliant industrial operations; the risk this rung guards against is housing arriving because jobs are nearby, then housing politics removing the jobs.
- **Compatible reuse of declining light-industrial land**, using the same underutilized-parcel logic already used for big-box retail and stadium parking in the Node Typologies framework, applied to industrial transition zones that no longer operate at capacity.

**Function served:** shortening the physical distance between where people live and where people work, directly reversing the postwar zoning decision to mandate that separation everywhere, regardless of whether it served anyone.

## What This Is Not

This is not a mandate to densify every neighborhood. Rung 0 is a legitimate, permanent, protected condition for any jurisdiction that opts not to climb further. This is not a war on cars or car infrastructure: car ownership, driving, and parking remain fully available at every rung, and what changes is that driving stops being the *only* option. This is not a single national architectural or zoning code: every rung operates through the same opt-in, pattern-book-governed, fiscally-gated mechanisms already established across this domain. This is not blanket upzoning imposed from outside: every rung requires local opt-in, and higher rungs remain concentrated at nodes and corridors identified through the existing trigger and eligibility criteria, not applied uniformly across a jurisdiction.

---

## Strategic Outcome

If deployed as intended, this ladder does not produce a single uniform outcome, and it is not a bet that every neighborhood should end up looking the same. It produces a range of legitimate outcomes: an unchanged single-family block with a legal ADU, a walkable missing-middle corridor, a transit-anchored mixed-use node with structured parking and active ground floors, an industrial-adjacent workforce district. Each is chosen by the jurisdiction and market that builds it, and each is judged by the same test: does this place let the people in it meet their daily needs, with a car or without one, closer to where they already live.

That is the function zoning was always supposed to serve. This ladder is an attempt to make it legal to serve it again.
