# Research Review: Civic Infrastructure — Governance, Public Space, and Community Pillars

**Source reviewed:** Greenspan, Elizabeth, and Randall Mason. *Civic Infrastructure: Sustaining and Sharing the Value of Parks, Libraries, and Other Civic Assets*. PennPraxis / William Penn Foundation, June 2018.  
**FP briefs covered:** Built Environment & Community Pillars (L1) · Community Stabilization Framework · Neighborhood Civic Overlay · Land Use Stabilization & Inclusive Growth  
**Prepared:** 2026-08-04

---

## Source Scope

**What this source addresses:** How civic assets (parks, libraries, rec centers, public plazas, fieldhouses) are successfully redeveloped and sustained in US cities. The central question is: what governance arrangements enable civic asset investment to produce equitable, durable outcomes? The report draws on eight case studies across sites (Garfield Park Chicago, Buchanan Mall SF, Eastern Coachella Valley), systems (Community Parks Initiative NYC, Neighborhood Park Plan Minneapolis), and policies (11th Street Bridge DC, Bayou 2020 Houston, LA River). It introduces a site/system/policy scale framework and a governance audit matrix as practical tools.

**What this source explicitly does not address:** The report does not provide causal effect estimates for crime reduction, health outcomes, or social capital formation from civic asset investment — it is a qualitative governance framework, not a causal identification study. It does not address federal implementation, execution corps–style deployment, or the specific instruments (STZs, UPI, SSI, DoDA) that FP uses to operationalize investment. It does not evaluate the community stabilization brief's environmental intervention components (lighting, lot remediation, code enforcement) — those are outside its scope.

**Implication for this review:** This source speaks most directly to the Built Environment & Community Pillars theoretical brief (L1) and the Neighborhood Civic Overlay (L4). Its findings are relevant but secondary to the Community Stabilization brief and Land Use Stabilization brief — useful for specific claims within those documents, not as a primary validation source for them.

## Aligned Findings

**1. The hard/soft integration thesis is consistent with FP's community pillar theory.**  
PennPraxis defines civic infrastructure as encompassing both physical assets and "the habits, traditions, management, and other social, political, and cultural processes that bring them to life — two realms that, together, constitute a whole." (p. 29) This is directly consistent with the Built Environment & Community Pillars brief's core claim: that physical space is a *necessary but not sufficient* condition for community formation. The brief argues that co-presence and repeated interaction require shared physical space; PennPraxis provides the complementary argument that physical space without social infrastructure does not sustain community. Both documents arrive at the same architectural conclusion through different theoretical paths. *Note: PennPraxis reaches this from a governance and planning practice tradition; the FP brief reaches it from a psychological stability and existential grounding theory. The convergence strengthens the claim but the two are independently grounded.*

**2. Governance as the determinative factor is consistent with FP's Execution Corps / DoDA architecture.**  
PennPraxis's central finding is that governance — not design quality, not funding volume, not civic engagement alone — is "the determinative factor" in whether civic asset investment produces durable, equitable outcomes. (p. 33) "Without effective governance, even the best design and policy ideas with ample resources stand little chance of being implemented, let alone sustained." (p. 81) This is consistent with FP's design principle that institutional execution capacity (Execution Corps, DoDA tracking, RMC blocker resolution) is the limiting constraint on policy success — not policy design quality. FP's operating system layer (L2) is an attempt to solve exactly the governance problem PennPraxis identifies.

**3. The site/system/policy multi-scale framework is consistent with FP's layered investment architecture.**  
PennPraxis finds that projects operating coherently across all three scales — site investment, system coordination, and policy alignment — consistently outperform those optimizing for a single scale. (p. 80) This is consistent with FP's STZ → NCO → community stabilization layered design: STZ provides the policy-scale framework, the NCO creates system-scale by-right permissions, and community stabilization funds site-level activation. The PennPraxis finding provides empirical support for why FP's multi-scale design is likely necessary — not just elegant. *Note: PennPraxis's evidence for this is qualitative case-study based, not experimental. The claim is plausible and well-illustrated but not causally established.*

**4. Maintenance as equal to capital investment is consistent with FP's failure modes analysis.**  
PennPraxis states explicitly: "Maintenance deserves equal weight, investment, and praise for sustaining the value of civic infrastructure." (p. 30) Buchanan Mall (SF) and Garfield Park (Chicago) are documented as cases where deferred maintenance created conditions requiring far costlier reinvestment. This is consistent with the Community Stabilization brief's failure modes section ("investments without maintenance" as the most common failure mode) and the NCO's fiscal sustainability requirement (projects must demonstrate ongoing maintenance revenue, not only capital funding).

**5. Anti-displacement must be an explicit governance requirement, not an assumed byproduct.**  
The High Line (NYC) is cited as a case where successful civic asset redevelopment generated "rapid rise in land values, intense tourism, and change of neighborhood character" — capturing the benefits for higher-income users and surrounding property owners rather than original residents. (p. 5) The 11th Street Bridge (DC) is cited as a counter-design where equity was built into governance from the start. PennPraxis's conclusion: anti-displacement outcomes require intentional governance design, not hope. This is consistent with FP's Land Use Stabilization brief's central argument but provides a civic-asset-specific empirical grounding for it.

## Gaps

*Topics within PennPraxis's scope — civic asset governance and redevelopment outcomes — where the source is nonetheless silent or weaker than expected.*

**1. Effect sizes on social outcomes are not provided.**  
PennPraxis makes strong claims about civic infrastructure's contribution to community formation, collective efficacy, and equitable urban development but provides no quantified effect estimates. The report is explicitly a governance framework and practitioner guide, not a causal study. For FP briefs that make specific claims about community formation effects — particularly the Built Environment & Community Pillars brief's collective efficacy chain — this source can support the mechanism logic but cannot provide the effect size evidence. Separate causal literature is needed.

**2. The governance typology is descriptive, not prescriptive.**  
The report identifies what governance arrangements worked in eight cases but does not specify design rules for replicating them. It describes the Garfield Park three-organization structure (Park District + Conservatory Alliance + Community Council) as effective but does not explain what made that specific combination generative or how to design toward it in a new context. FP's NCO and community stabilization framework need more than "governance matters" — they need governance design rules. This source supports the problem diagnosis; the solution design requires additional sources or FP's own specification work.

**3. Federal-scale implementation is entirely outside this source's frame.**  
All eight case studies are municipal or philanthropic implementations. The source says nothing about whether federal coordination enhances, complicates, or is neutral to the local governance dynamics it identifies as essential. This is the same gap noted in the 17A review, and it remains the most significant unresolved question for FP's entire community investment architecture.

## Divergences

**1. The ecosystem analogy implies emergent rather than designed governance — FP assumes designed.**  
PennPraxis repeatedly invokes an ecosystem metaphor: civic infrastructure is "a resilient system of spaces and processes" in which "relationships between factors are as significant as the factors themselves." (p. 30) The ecosystem framing implies that effective governance arrangements emerge from adaptive relationships over time — the Garfield Park three-organization structure evolved over a decade in response to changing conditions, it was not designed from the start.

FP's Execution Corps deployment model assumes governance can be designed, specified, and stood up in 90 days via a structured protocol. The CRC model specifies roles, cadences, and accountability mechanisms in advance.

*FP position:* The tension is real but manageable. The EC/CRC model is designed to establish the minimum structural conditions (weekly cadence, accountability mechanism, data targeting) and then exit — leaving the local ecosystem to evolve. FP is designing the scaffold, not the building. Whether a 90-day federal deployment can establish durable governance rather than temporary compliance is an open empirical question this source raises but cannot answer.

**2. PennPraxis emphasizes civic engagement as the most common barrier to progress; FP emphasizes institutional execution.**  
PennPraxis identifies "deficits of engagement" as "perhaps the most-common barrier to progress" across its case studies. (p. 82) The Garfield Park Community Council's founding purpose was to organize residents to advocate for their neighborhood — community organizing as governance infrastructure.

FP's community stabilization brief is primarily an institutional execution framework. Community input is listed as a design requirement and a failure mode if absent ("investment without community ownership"), but the brief's primary focus is the coordination architecture (CRC cadence, DoDA targeting, EC deployment), not community organizing infrastructure.

*FP position:* These may be emphasizing different failure modes for different program types. PennPraxis studied long-running municipal reinvestment projects where the primary bottleneck was sustained civic engagement. FP's community stabilization intervention is shorter-term (90–180 day visible improvement) and environmentally focused — the engagement requirement is real but secondary to the coordination problem the brief is designed to solve. However, the brief could be stronger on what community engagement looks like in a federal EC deployment and how it connects to ongoing local advocacy structures after the EC exits.

## Open Questions

**1. Can a federal EC deployment establish durable local governance rather than temporary compliance?**  
PennPraxis's case evidence suggests effective civic asset governance develops over years through adaptive relationships. FP's CRC model is designed to be deployed in 90 days and exit within 18–36 months. Does the EC deployment create conditions under which local governance matures, or does it create a dependency that collapses when the EC leaves? This is the most important implementation question the PennPraxis framework raises for FP.

**2. What is the right governance structure for FP's third-spaces investment after EC exit?**  
PennPraxis's Garfield Park case shows a specific three-organization structure that evolved to sustain a complex civic asset. FP's community stabilization brief funds third spaces and activates them but does not specify the post-EC governance structure. What entity owns, programs, and maintains an activated community center after the EC exits? The NCO framework creates regulatory permission; it does not create organizational capacity. This is a gap the brief should eventually address.

**3. At what investment density do anti-gentrification effects become significant?**  
PennPraxis's High Line case shows that a single high-profile civic investment can generate significant land value increases and displacement pressure. FP's community stabilization framework operates at 20–50 micro-sites per city using existing city resources — likely below the High Line's scale. But federally-funded investment at scale could cross the threshold where anti-gentrification governance becomes critical. The PennPraxis framework doesn't specify this threshold; neither does FP. The Land Use Stabilization brief addresses the policy mechanism but not the trigger conditions.

**4. Does the NCO's fiscal sustainability test adequately capture maintenance requirements?**  
PennPraxis's maintenance finding is strong: deferred maintenance consistently produces worse long-term outcomes than sustained lower-level investment. The NCO requires projects to demonstrate ongoing maintenance revenue — but the fiscal test is designed to assess new infrastructure impacts, not to model full lifecycle maintenance costs. Is the NCO's solvency test sufficient to prevent the maintenance failures PennPraxis documents?

## Design Decisions & Editorial Rationale

### Decision 1: Primary brief targets are Built Environment (L1) and NCO, not Community Stabilization

**Decision made:** This review is filed as primary for the Built Environment and NCO briefs and secondary for Community Stabilization. The source is cited as corroborating evidence for community stabilization claims (maintenance, governance) but not as a primary validation source.

**Source position:** PennPraxis is explicitly a governance framework for parks, libraries, and similar civic assets — not an environmental crime reduction study. Its claims about community formation are theoretical and qualitative, not experimentally derived.

**FP position:** The Built Environment brief and NCO are the natural primary targets; Community Stabilization uses this source for corroboration only.

**Justification:** Using a qualitative governance framework as the primary evidence for a crime reduction brief would overextend the source. PennPraxis says nothing about crime outcomes. Its governance typology is genuinely relevant to Community Stabilization's post-EC design gap, but that's a secondary application. Matching source to brief at the right level of scope is better practice than treating every relevant source as a primary validator.
